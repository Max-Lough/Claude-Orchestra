# Critique

## Steelman

The plan replaces a model-named roster with a nineteen-code canonical task taxonomy held in strict bijection with nineteen capability roles, so routing becomes a two-step function: classify the intake once by its acceptance artifact and dominant difficulty, then read the cast off that class's role contract, with risk (T0–T3) carried as an orthogonal modifier rather than as extra classes. Quality is enforced mechanically instead of by convention — deterministic evidence first, a fresh-context reviewer for every semantic source change, an opposite-vendor reviewer selected from *recorded author family* for every consequential artifact, and a human gate for irreversible actions — and the plan deliberately refuses to trade that independence invariant against allowance pressure, stating that an exhausted pool must produce `WAITING_FOR_INDEPENDENT_REVIEW` rather than a correlated approval. Cost is denominated in three non-convertible per-vendor allowance units (OAU/AAU/GAU) governed by a four-state pool scheduler with a 25% review reserve, and the whole architecture is staged behind a ten-step validation programme that freezes live access facts first, shadows the new router against the current harness on fifty real tasks, and canaries by risk tier before any new role receives mutation authority.

That is a coherent and disciplined design. The class↔role bijection is the cleanest available answer to done-criterion 2, the author-family-driven review matrix is the cleanest available answer to done-criterion 4, and attaching a numeric *reversal condition* to every adjudication is a better epistemic habit than most planning documents manage.

## Findings

### 1. [BLOCKER] The mandatory cross-vendor review invariant is never checked for throughput, and its only non-human relief valve is disqualified by the plan's own role contract.

*Target: "Review and escalation protocol → Mandatory versus preferred independence"; "Pool-exhaustion behavior"; "Whole-system acceptance gates".*

The plan mandates opposite-vendor review for every semantic source change and every T2/T3 artifact, and states the invariant "has no allowance-based downgrade. Only a human-authorized policy change may relax it." It then quotes, as its own cost basis, the Codex per-window figure "Sol: approximately 10–100 local messages on the entry subscription tier," and reserves "at least 25% of each premium vendor pool… for reviews and incident work."

Run the plan's own arithmetic. Eight of its nineteen roles author on Anthropic (O0, A0, I0, I1, E2, E3, E6, D0), and every semantic artifact from all eight must be closed by a Sol review. On the entry tier the 25% floor guarantees roughly 2.5–25 Sol *messages* per five-hour window — and an agentic review that re-reads a diff, re-runs the verification manifest and writes a severity-tagged verdict is not one message. On the low end of the vendor range the plan itself cites, the guaranteed review capacity is under one complete review per window. The document never computes reviews-per-window, never states which subscription tier the design assumes (it appears only as an open question: "Which Claude, ChatGPT, and Google subscription tiers are actually installed on the target machine?"), and none of its fifteen whole-system acceptance gates is a throughput or liveness gate.

The failure this invites is not a quality failure — it is a stall. When the OpenAI window empties, all Anthropic-authored work queues indefinitely as `WAITING_FOR_INDEPENDENT_REVIEW`. The plan's degradation ladder names exactly one non-human escape: "use an activated third vendor." That third vendor is Gemini in seat M0, which the plan (a) disables by default, (b) gates behind an activation test consisting of "at least twenty **multimodal** tasks," which measures nothing about code-review competence, and (c) explicitly forbids from code judgment in M0's own `Must not receive` list ("general coding, final UI implementation, final architectural judgment"). So the ladder's only non-human rung cannot legally be climbed. Every pool exhaustion therefore terminates at a human, on a design whose own risk table already flags "Human bottleneck."

Note also that the acceptance gate "Pool-exhaustion tests never yield false approval or same-family substitution" passes trivially when nothing runs: it measures safety and is silent on liveness, which is precisely the property under threat. The plan's "Review bottleneck" risk row *names* this hazard, but its response ("maintain 25% reserves, compress packets, batch only independent artifacts, and queue rather than weaken the gate") is a set of mitigations with no quantity attached to any of them and no test that would fail if they were insufficient.

### 2. [BLOCKER] The Director is the only premium seat with no declared fallback cast, so Anthropic exhaustion halts orchestration entirely.

*Target: "Role catalog → 1. Director"; "Pool-exhaustion behavior"; "Assumptions and open questions".*

A0 declares "the declared fallback is Opus 5 high." E3 declares "fallback Opus 5 high." M0 declares a Fable fallback. E2 declares Terra as "the declared depletion fallback." The Director declares none — its casting line reads "Claude Opus 5 at medium effort; high only when classifying genuinely ambiguous or irreversible work," and its routing-table escalation path ("Opus high → Systems Architect → human") stays inside the Anthropic pool at every rung.

Under the plan's own scheduler, `Exhausted` means "no new calls." Because the Director is the sole owner of canonical classification, permission decisions, author *and reviewer* family selection, work-graph ownership, integration sequencing and human escalation, an exhausted Anthropic pool does not degrade the system — it stops it. Nothing can even be *queued*, because queueing requires classification, and classification is a Director act.

The plan half-notices this: its open questions ask "Is the current Director guard expected to support mid-session model switching, or should a model change terminate and restart the orchestration session?" That is the adjacent mechanical question. The architectural question — *what plays the Director when its pool is empty* — is never asked, though the document resolves harder questions elsewhere. Done-criterion 3 and the DEPLOYMENT AND COST BASIS section ask explicitly "what happens when one vendor's pool is exhausted mid-session and the harness must degrade"; for the system's most-invoked seat, the plan's answer is that it does not.

The fix is small (declare a Sol-medium Director mirror, state the hand-off artifact, and state which decisions the mirror may not take alone given that it then shares a family with half the roster's authors) — but its absence is load-bearing, because every other degradation path in the document assumes a Director exists to execute it.

### 3. [MAJOR] The OpenAI reviewer casting rests on an authoring benchmark read as a review benchmark, and the report contains the review benchmark it should have used.

*Target: "Approach → Evidence base and adjudications", row "Sol versus Terra for review"; every AR row in the routing table.*

The plan states: "The reported CodeRabbit clean-pass rates are 63.7% for Sol and 40.7% for Terra," and concludes "Sol, not Terra, is the OpenAI flagship reviewer. Terra may screen structured or low-risk material but cannot close a consequential gate."

Verified against the tree. `research/openai-models.md:85` lists "CodeRabbit 100-task repo suite | **63.7%** clean-pass (no trial errors) | long-horizon, messy multi-file repos"; `:289` gives Terra 40.7% on the same suite; `:310` glosses it as Terra's cliff on "deep, messy, long-horizon repo work," where CodeRabbit "describes it reaching answers 'by a more heuristic path.'" That is an **agentic authoring** suite. It measures whether a model can complete a messy multi-file repository task without trial errors — which is why the same report cites it in Terra's *"Where Terra breaks"* section about scoped versus split-resistant orders, not in any review discussion.

The report's actual review-quality measurement is a different instrument: a **105-task review suite** (`research/openai-models.md:121–123`), on which "Sol caught 65/105 actionable issues (Claude Opus 4.8 and the human baseline: 66) and led on full review-task passes (74 vs 72)." It reports **no Terra figure at all**.

Two failures follow. First, the discriminating evidence for reviewer casting is inverted in kind: an authoring score is used to exclude a model from a reviewing role, while the report's genuine reviewer datum — Sol at rough parity with an Anthropic flagship and with the human baseline — is never cited and never used, even though it is the strongest available support for the plan's own cross-vendor mandate. Second, the reversal condition the plan attaches ("Terra may close routine gates only if an internal seeded-defect evaluation narrows the **major-defect recall gap** to under 5 points") presupposes a measured recall gap that does not exist in any supplied source, so the condition can neither be satisfied nor falsified as written.

The consequence compounds Finding 1: excluding Terra forces every OpenAI-side review onto Sol, the pool's most allowance-hungry seat by the plan's own weights (17–25 OAU against Terra's 8–10), roughly doubling to tripling the draw of the mandate that Finding 1 shows is already the binding constraint. A Terra-medium review lane for T1 changes, gated by seeded-defect measurement, is exactly the relief valve the design needs — and the plan closes it on a misread number.

### 4. [MAJOR] The cost model's two tables contradict each other: several per-class estimates are smaller than one turn of the model assigned to that class.

*Target: "Cost model → Normalized allowance units" and the per-class estimate table.*

The unit table defines 1 OAU as "one bounded Luna-medium local work-order turn" and sets "approximate equal-volume weights of Luna 1, Terra 8–10, and Sol 17–25 OAU." The derivation is sound — the quoted per-window ranges (Sol 10–100, Terra 25–200, Luna 250–2,000) do imply Luna : Terra : Sol ≈ 1 : 10 : 20–25 — and the plan deserves credit for deriving it rather than importing a price table.

It then does not use it. The per-class table gives:

- E0 Terminal Operator (Sol **high**): 10–30 OAU
- N1 Deep Researcher (Sol medium): 8–20 OAU
- E5 UI Engineer (Sol high): 10–25 OAU
- E7 Security Engineer (Sol high): 15–35 OAU
- E4 Data Engineer (Terra **high**): 8–20 OAU

At the low end, four of those five are *below the cost of a single turn* of the model performing them, and high-effort turns should cost more than the baseline weight the ranges were derived from, not less. An entire terminal-operations task cannot cost half a Sol turn.

The failure this invites is that the cost model cannot do the job it was built for. The whole-system gate "Premium allowance per accepted T1 task is at least 35% below an all-frontier baseline" is not computable from figures that contradict the unit definition; a scheduler seeded with the class table will systematically under-reserve on the OpenAI pool, which is the pool Finding 1 shows is already tight; and the "Main economy" column's claims ("Sol's terminal completion rate avoids repeated lower-tier loops") cannot be checked against numbers that do not close. The unit table appears correct and the class table appears to have been written independently of it.

### 5. [MAJOR] No frequency mix is ever stated, so "share of each vendor's allowance consumed" — the quantity the cost basis explicitly asks for — is never computed; and the plan's own role assignment loads one pool on both sides of the ledger.

*Target: "Cost model → Where savings come from" and the per-class table.*

The DEPLOYMENT AND COST BASIS section asks the cost model to reason in terms of "share of each vendor's allowance consumed per task class… how work is balanced across two (or three) independent pools." The plan supplies per-task draw and a borrowed health tripwire (director + ceiling above 70% of normalized consumption is unhealthy — accurately reproduced from `research/dossier_both.md:434`), but never assigns expected call frequencies to its nineteen classes. Per-task draw without a mix does not yield a share, so the required quantity is absent.

That gap hides a specific imbalance the plan asserts away rather than tests. Its E2 adjudication row justifies Sonnet-as-primary partly because it "leaves OpenAI capacity available for mandatory reviews" — a genuinely good pool-balance argument. But the rest of the roster pushes the other way: OpenAI authors N0, N1, N2, E0, E1, E4, E5 and E7, *and* reviews all eight Anthropic-authored classes, with Sol — its costliest seat — serving as both the default reviewer and the primary author of four classes. Most consequentially, the plan moves the single highest-frequency role in any harness, repository lookup, *off* the Anthropic pool (where the current `scout` sits on Haiku, verified at `agents/scout.md`) and *onto* the OpenAI pool as N0/Luna, where it competes with the review reserve. Luna is cheap per call, but the plan's own delta rationale — "Luna's allowance economics are strongest for bounded retrieval" — compares across two pools that the same section declares non-convertible ("No cross-model or cross-vendor conversion is claimed"), so the comparison that motivates the swap is not one the plan's own unit system permits.

The failure invited: the pool that must fund the entire de-correlation mandate is also the pool doing most of the authoring, and nothing in the design would reveal that until shadow mode at Step 8.

### 6. [MAJOR] The "deterministic verifier sidecar" is a node in the topology and the sole reviewer in several routing rows, but it has no catalog entry, no owner, and no place in the work plan.

*Target: "Hierarchy and topology" diagram; "Role catalog"; routing rows N0, N1, E1, R0; "Where savings come from"; work plan Steps 3–6.*

The component carries real weight. N0's reviewer is "a machine checker [that] verifies that every cited path, line, symbol, commit, or URL exists." N1's is a "citation checker." R0's own verdicts are audited by "machine-verified" evidence, checkout identity, tree audit and nonce. E1 closes on "deterministic transformation checks [that] remain authoritative." The savings section rests on it: "omitting model review is acceptable only when a deterministic check proves the entire acceptance condition." Q0 depends on "deterministic mutation and flake testing." Step 4's verification depends on "seeded artifacts containing correctness, security, scope, and test defects."

Yet the sidecar is specified nowhere. It has no purpose sentence, no tool surface, no failure modes, no statement of whether it is a model or code, and no answer to the recursion it creates: a checker is itself a semantic source change, which under this plan's own invariant requires an author role, an independent test designer and an opposite-vendor review — and the work plan never assigns that construction to a class or a step. Nor does the plan say what happens when no deterministic oracle exists for an artifact type, which is the common case for prose, design and architecture artifacts that the plan nonetheless routes through "machine evidence audit."

Either the sidecar is a role — in which case the document's central structural claim (nineteen classes, nineteen roles, each with the nine required fields) is wrong, and the Step 3 schema test that "asserts all nineteen classes occur exactly once as primary ownership" would not catch a twentieth un-catalogued participant — or it is tooling, in which case the topology diagram lists a non-role as a child of the Director and the work plan has an unbudgeted build. Either way, the cheapest closure path in the architecture, where most of the claimed savings live, rests on an unspecified component.

### 7. [MAJOR] Q0 has no trigger, so under the one-class rule the implementer usually writes its own oracle — the exact correlation the plan's risk table claims to have mitigated.

*Target: "Canonical task and risk taxonomy" ("Each intake receives exactly one task class"); "Role catalog → 16. Independent Test Designer"; role 10's tool surface; risk row "Test correlation".*

Q0's purpose is "an oracle independent of the implementation author," and its cast is chosen by the independence rule (Terra for Anthropic-authored implementation, Sonnet for OpenAI-authored). The risk table promises the corresponding mitigation: "Opposite-family test author, black-box-first drafting, mutation testing, and independent review."

But a feature request classifies as E2 or E3, not Q0 — the taxonomy assigns exactly one class per intake, and E2's own tool surface includes "local tests" while E3's includes "tests, build tools." Nothing in the document states when the Director *must* spawn a companion Q0 task, which risk tier compels it, whether Q0 is mandatory at T2, or how the two orders are sequenced (Q0's contract says the implementation diff "is withheld until black-box tests are drafted when practical," which implies Q0 runs first or in parallel — but no routing rule creates it). The cost table's "Usually absorbed by the implementation gate" is the only statement on the subject and it is a budget note, not a rule.

The failure invited is precise and familiar: in practice Q0 fires only when a human thinks to ask for it, the implementer writes its own tests, the tests encode the implementer's misreading of the spec, and the mandatory opposite-vendor reviewer inherits a green suite that proves the wrong thing. This is structurally the same defect the plan correctly diagnoses in the current harness — a correct convention that nothing enforces — reintroduced one level down.

### 8. [MAJOR] Sequencing: the design's riskiest assumption is not tested until Steps 7–8, after four steps of specification have been built on top of it.

*Target: "Work plan" Steps 1–8.*

Step 1 is well chosen for what it covers — freezing live access facts and testing "Fable and Gemini paths first because they are the riskiest availability assumptions" is exactly right for *availability* risk, and the stop condition is real.

But availability is not the riskiest assumption in this design. The riskiest assumption is that two subscription pools can sustain mandatory double review at the per-window message ranges the plan itself quotes (Finding 1). That assumption is not measured until the casting trials (Step 7) and shadow mode (Step 8, "at least fifty real tasks") — by which point Step 3 has encoded the registry, Step 4 has encoded the review policy *around the mandate*, Step 5 has encoded the scheduler and reserves, and Step 6 has produced a full implementation map across `ORCHESTRA.md`, four agent directories, hooks, skills, configuration, installers and tests. If the mandate proves throughput-infeasible on the installed tier, all four steps are re-worked, and the plan has no stop condition anywhere that would halt on that discovery — every stop condition it does define concerns Fable access, Gemini activation, or benchmark disputes.

A cheap probe exists and is runnable today, before Step 3: the current harness already supports forcing a cross-vendor review pass (`reviewEngine: "codex"` or `"dual"` in `.claude/orchestra.json`, documented in `README.md`), so twenty real changes can be pushed through the mandatory double-review path this week and the per-pool draw and time-to-throttle measured directly. Moving that probe to Step 2 costs days and de-risks the four most expensive specification steps. As written, the plan validates its cheapest assumptions first and its most expensive one last.

### 9. [MAJOR] N0's hard filter is defined on a quantity that is unknowable at classification time, so the cheapest and highest-frequency seat is guarded by an unenforceable rule.

*Target: "Role catalog → 3. Repository Scout" (`Must not receive: … more than approximately 100,000 relevant tokens`); risk row "Luna context failure" ("Hard N2 threshold"); the N0/N2 discriminators.*

Relevance is discovered by searching, not declared before it. A dispatcher can bound the context it *supplies*; it cannot bound the context a bounded-sounding question turns out to *require*. So Luna is handed a 5K-token packet and a question whose honest answer depends on evidence spread across 300K tokens, and the documented consequence follows: MRCR 41.3% against Terra's 89.6% (verified at `research/dossier_both.md:129` and `:224` — "Luna cannot be trusted to retrieve a fact from a large haystack") produces a fluent, well-formatted answer with the needle missing.

The plan's stated safeguards do not reach this case. The "machine citation checker" verifies that every cited path, line and symbol *exists* — and every one will, because the failure is not fabrication but omission; what is missing is the path nobody cited. The escalation rule ("escalates after one incomplete search pass") requires the scout to *know* its pass was incomplete, which is exactly the metacognitive capability the MRCR number says this cast lacks. And the plan compounds the exposure by moving the highest-frequency recon role onto this cast.

A workable form of the same rule exists and the plan does not adopt it: constrain the *supplied* context (which is enforceable), require the scout to return its search surface — globs run, files opened, files truncated — so a machine can detect a search that terminated on a truncation rather than an exhaustion, and treat any truncation as an automatic N2 reclassification. As written, the constraint is prose the dispatcher cannot evaluate.

### 10. [MAJOR] E4 casts as its primary the model the same entry names as weak at exactly E4's dominant difficulty.

*Target: "Role catalog → 12. Data and Schema Engineer".*

The class discriminator is "Data shape, integrity, transactional behavior, or migration safety dominates." The casting rationale is "Structured reasoning, long-context handling, workhorse economics." The weaknesses, four lines later, are "Reported code-smell and vulnerability misses; may under-model rollback, locking, partial failure, or real data skew."

The stated strength (long-context recall, MRCR 89.6) is not the dominant difficulty of migration engineering; the stated weakness *is*. The plan's own P4 says "A model's known context cliff, review miss pattern, over-agency, refusal behavior, or tendency to over-engineer is encoded in `must_not_receive` and escalation rules, not left as prose" — and here the failure mode is left as prose, inside the entry that assigns the work. P12 reserves irreversible authority for humans, which is correct but orthogonal: the human approves the *execution*, having been handed a migration whose rollback path was authored by the roster's weakest rollback modeller. The compensating control ("Opus 5 high reviews semantics and rollback") is applied downstream of the design decisions that a review can only accept or reject wholesale.

Available evidence points the other way for the *authoring* seat: `research/dossier_both.md:289` records Opus 5 as "most careful about irreversible side effects" with the lowest measured misaligned-behaviour score, and lists "large refactors" and "final technical integration" among its strengths. If the objection is allowance cost, that is an argument the plan should make explicitly — casting the irreversibility-adjacent seat on economics is a real tradeoff and the entry states it nowhere.

### 11. [MAJOR] M0's declared fallback is the roster's most rationed seat, contradicts the plan's own allowance rule, and is unsupported for the modalities that define the role.

*Target: "Role catalog → 18. Multimodal Reference Analyst"; routing row M0; "Pool-exhaustion behavior"; the M0 cost row.*

M0's purpose is to "convert image, video, audio, and PDF source material into a cited, implementation-ready evidence specification" — high-volume, low-judgment reading. Its declared fallback is "Fable 5 high."

Three problems, each verifiable inside the document. First, it contradicts an explicit rule the plan states two sections later: "Fable's separately constrained weekly allowance is not used for routine tasks." Bulk reference extraction is the most routine work in the roster. Second, it contradicts P3 ("The cheapest proven cast owns the volume") by picking the *most* expensive cast for the cheapest class; the plan's own cost row concedes the size of the error — "2–8 GAU" for the Gemini path against "12–30 AAU plus Sol review" for the fallback — without treating the concession as a reason to choose differently. Third, and decisively, the plan supplies no evidence anywhere that Fable accepts video or audio input, while it does claim exactly that capability for the Gemini cast ("native text/image/video/audio/PDF handling"). A fallback that cannot process two of the four modalities in the role's own purpose statement is not a fallback for that role; it is a fallback for the subset of the role it happens to cover. (The supplied reports are silent on video and audio for every model — I checked all three — so this is an unsupported claim in the plan rather than a contradicted one.)

The failure invited: when the Gemini activation gate fails — which the plan itself rates as likely enough to disable the seat by default — M0 either silently narrows to still-image and PDF work with nobody noticing the video and audio classes have no owner, or it burns the weekly-rationed Fable allowance on transcription. Opus 5 and Terra are both credited with multimodal competence in the supplied evidence (`research/openai-models.md:287,305`, MMMU-Pro 80.7 for Terra) and either would be a defensible cheap mirror; the plan considers neither.

### 12. [MAJOR] The deltas table omits the one component in the current tree whose transport violates the plan's own subscription constraint.

*Target: "Deltas from the current roster"; "Assumptions" ("No API-only models, provider API keys, or metered per-token paths are required by the default design").*

The delta table is thorough — it covers `scout`, `detective`, `executor`, both heavy tiers, `reviewer`, the codex executor and reviewer roles, `specialists/modeler`, the three cross-plan roles, `.claude/orchestra.json`, the guard, the dual rosters, the installer, the transport-integrity mechanics, and the skills. It does not cover `planner-gpt` or the `/deep-plan` skill.

Verified in the tree: `packs/codex/agents/planner-gpt.md` and `.claude/skills/deep-plan/` both exist, and the lane's runner `packs/codex/hooks/orchestra-deepplan.js` posts to `https://api.openai.com` `/v1/responses` using `OPENAI_API_KEY` (see lines 55, 77–78, 116–117, 288–289, 316; line 55 reads "`OPENAI_API_KEY` required; the consultation bills to it"). This is a metered per-token path inside the reference implementation.

The plan's adjudication section shows it understood the constraint precisely — "The standard OpenAI path is Codex CLI authentication with a ChatGPT subscription, not an API key. API-key use would create a separately billed dependency outside this design" — but it never audited the tree for an existing instance, and its assumption list then asserts the design requires no such path without qualifying that the harness it is replacing contains one. The brief's deltas section exists precisely to audit the current harness, and the brief's deployment constraint says API dependencies must be "explicitly flag[ged]" with "what it would take to satisfy it." A reader implementing this delta table would carry a metered API lane into an architecture that claims to have none, and the plan's `planner-gpt` role would land in the new taxonomy with no class, no cast and no review policy.

### 13. [MINOR] Reviewer blinding is asserted as a benefit while the information it destroys is used by everyone except the reviewer, and the tradeoff is never stated.

*Target: "Hierarchy and topology → task packet" ("`author_family` is scheduler metadata and is omitted from the reviewer's prompt… The reviewer sees the artifact, requirements, permitted scope, and evidence, but not vendor, model, effort, or price"); "Role catalog → 19", weakness "preference bias".*

P4 says characteristic failure modes should be encoded as routing constraints rather than left for a Director to remember. The plan then removes from the reviewer the single fact that would let it *target* those failure modes. Its own E0 and E7 entries justify heightened scrutiny of Sol-authored mutations on exactly that basis ("every Sol-authored mutation" is separately listed as requiring mandatory review "due to the reported integrity and over-agency risks") — so the scheduler acts on author identity while the reviewer, the party best placed to act on it, is denied it.

No evidence is offered that model-identity preference bias exists in reviewers, and none of the three supplied reports measures it; the mitigation is hypothesised, the cost is real. The tradeoff may still be worth making, but the plan should state it — and should consider the middle option it skips: blind the reviewer to *vendor and price* while handing it the author's characteristic-failure checklist as an unattributed "known hazards for this artifact class" section, which preserves the anti-bias intent and restores the targeting signal.

### 14. [MINOR] A genuine disagreement between two supplied reports on interactive-UI work is resolved without being registered, and two of the plan's best-grounded castings carry no citation.

*Target: "Approach → adjudications", row "Sol's role"; "Role catalog → 13"; "Role catalog → 14".*

`research/cross_vendor_agent_harness_roster_summary.md:269` names Opus 5 the "visual-systems builder," and its strong-assignment list at `:287–300` includes "interactive UI systems" and "rendering-feedback loops" alongside procedural mesh generation, Blender Python, Godot procedural generation and shader work. `research/openai-models.md:181–187` gives Sol the Design Arena #1-band result for generated UIs. The plan casts E5 to Sol and E6 to Opus.

The split is defensible and, on E6, better-supported than the document lets on: the roster summary's Opus list matches E6's scope almost item for item, and the same report's Fable section (`:231–244`, "spatial reconstruction from multiple references," "interpreting dense visual or geometric information") is direct support for the M0 fallback the plan chose. But neither entry cites any of it — E6's rationale ("Visual-systems construction, technical debugging, multi-object reasoning") is the only casting rationale in the catalog with no number and no source behind it — and the adjudication table, whose whole function is to record report conflicts, never registers that one report assigns interactive UI systems to Opus while the plan assigns them to Sol. The brief makes adjudication a hard constraint. As written, two well-grounded decisions read as coin flips.

### 15. [MINOR] Classifier fallback is encoded as a Fable-only hazard, but the mandatory reviewer of every security artifact is Opus, which the same source implicates.

*Target: "Role catalog → 2" (weaknesses); "Role catalog → 15" (review row); risk row "Hidden model routing".*

`research/dossier_both.md:36` states that classifier fallback affects "Fable 5 (**and sometimes Opus 5**)," and `:260` notes that benign security engineering — "CVE discussion, hardening, 'write a test that simulates XSS'" — "can eject you from Fable without the director noticing unless fallback is logged." The plan correctly encodes the hazard for Fable and correctly routes E7 authorship to Sol (matching `:127`, "Sol is the GA defensive-cyber worker"), but casts Opus 5 high as the mandatory cross-vendor reviewer of every E7 artifact without carrying the hazard across, and never lists classifier behaviour among Opus's weaknesses in the Director or reviewer entries.

The "Hidden model routing" mitigation — "log resolved model id and date per call; fail closed where identity cannot be established" — is the right instinct but does not necessarily reach a provider-side classifier substitution that leaves the reported model id unchanged. The failure invited is narrow but sharp: a security gate closed by an undisclosed weaker model, in the one class where the plan otherwise insists on a human co-signature.

### 16. [MINOR] Performance work has an investigator but no implementer, and the omission is never justified.

*Target: "Canonical task and risk taxonomy"; "Role catalog → 7" (I1 escalation: "confirmed fixes to the appropriate executor").*

The brief names performance work among the classes to consider and requires that additions be justified; it equally implies that a considered omission should be reasoned. I1 owns performance *investigation* and hands "confirmed fixes to the appropriate executor" — but the taxonomy offers no discriminator that routes a performance fix. E2 requires "a bounded specification exists," which performance work characteristically lacks (the specification is a target number, not a design); E3 requires crossing more than three subsystems, which many hot-path optimizations do not. A performance optimization therefore lands in the gap between two classes, which is exactly the ambiguity the class↔role bijection exists to eliminate, and the Step 2 corpus lists ambiguous boundaries to test (N0/I0, I0/I1, E1/E2, E2/E3, E5/E6, M0/E5) without including this one.

## Comparative assessment

### Where the plan under critique is stronger than my own draft

**1. Machine-readable contracts, written out.** Its task-packet and result-packet YAML schemas enumerate actual fields — `author_family`, `scope_allow`/`scope_deny`, `tree_identity`, `integrity_nonce`, `scope_variance`, `recommended_next_class`, `verification_commands`. My draft names four schemas (order, report, verdict, casting record) and describes their purpose without enumerating them. Against the brief's test — "specify the architecture in enough detail that someone else could write them without further decisions" — theirs is straightforwardly closer to executable. A reader could write the packet validator from their text and could not from mine.

**2. Reversal conditions bound to decisions rather than to numbers.** Every row of their adjudication table carries a numeric condition under which the decision flips ("recast if the internal accepted-result advantage is below 3 points or normalized allowance exceeds the alternative by more than 2×"). My draft has an evidence register with number sensitivity, which attaches uncertainty to *figures*; theirs attaches it to *choices*, which is the more actionable form and directly serves the brief's "state the number, its source, and how the casting would change if the number moved." I think this is the single best structural idea in their document and it is better than my equivalent.

**3. Risk as an orthogonal modifier.** Separating T0–T3 from the nineteen task classes turns the review policy into a two-dimensional function (author family × risk) that can be enumerated and exhaustively tested — and their Step 4 does exactly that ("a matrix test enumerates every author family × risk tier × artifact type… must find zero self-review routes and zero same-family T2/T3 routes"). My draft folds stakes into per-role prose and a gate-class notion, which is harder to test mechanically. Theirs is cleaner.

**4. A tighter roster with a machine-checkable bijection.** Nineteen classes to nineteen roles, with a schema test that asserts each class occurs exactly once as a primary. My twenty-four roles cover more ground and give each role primary/mirror/ceiling castings, but the one-to-one mapping in my draft lives in a table rather than in a load-time assertion, and a smaller roster genuinely has fewer boundary disputes to adjudicate. Done-criterion 2 asks for unambiguity, not breadth.

**5. Numeric topology limits.** Four default children and six at the cap; at most two concurrent allowance-hungry calls; two model levels below the Director; an explicit list of the seven leads permitted to delegate and the four classes permitted as Level-2 children. My topology is described but less crisply bounded. Their fan-out and depth rules could be enforced by a scheduler as written.

**6. Explicit whole-system acceptance gates.** Fifteen pass/fail lines, several with the honesty clause "statistical uncertainty is reported rather than rounded into a claim of parity." My verification is thorough but has fewer hard numeric thresholds. Finding 1 argues one critical gate is missing; the fourteen that are there are better stated than mine.

### Where my own draft is stronger

**1. Degradation is answered rather than deferred.** Under a cost basis whose defining feature is exhaustible per-vendor pools, the central design question is what the system does when a pool empties. Every role in my draft carries primary, mirror and (where relevant) ceiling castings, so exhaustion is a re-cast that leaves the contract, work order and acceptance criteria unchanged, and §5.6 walks the ladder rung by rung. The plan under critique gives fallbacks to A0, E3, E2 and M0 but not to the Director (Finding 2), and its review mandate has no non-human rung at all (Finding 1). Its refusal to weaken the independence invariant is admirable and I would keep it; the gap is that refusing to weaken a mandate obliges you to price it, and the pricing is absent.

**2. The cost arithmetic closes.** Both drafts independently derived the same OpenAI ratio from the same published per-window ranges (Sol : Terra : Luna ≈ 20–25 : 10 : 1), which is a genuinely encouraging convergence. Mine then applies it consistently — terminal operations at 220–550 OU reads as 10–25 Sol turns at ~22 OU each — while theirs produces class estimates below one turn of the assigned model (Finding 4). A cost model that contradicts its own unit is not usable for the scheduling decisions both drafts want to make.

**3. Benchmark provenance on the reviewer casting.** Mine separates the CodeRabbit 100-task authoring suite from the 105-task review suite and casts reviewers on the latter, with the METR reward-hacking finding handled structurally (independent re-run, nonce echo, tree audit) rather than by exclusion. Theirs conflates the two suites and excludes the cheap OpenAI reviewer on an authoring number (Finding 3) — which is both an evidence error and, given Finding 1, an expensive one.

**4. Two seats their roster does not staff.** A deterministic **Verifier** as a first-class role with a contract, a tool surface and failure modes — theirs relies on an unspecified sidecar (Finding 6) that half its cheap closure paths depend on — and a **Sweeper** that owns the "what did the parallel children miss" question after a fan-out. Their topology caps fan-out but assigns no one to the characteristic failure of narrow parallel orders, which is the gap between them.

**5. Live evidence that post-dates the supplied reports, used to change a casting.** All three reports route hard terminal work on Terminal-Bench 2.1, where five models sit inside a six-point band with acknowledged harness variance of similar magnitude. My draft adjudicates that against the Terminal-Bench 3.0 snapshot (retrieved 2026-08-27: Opus 5 42.7, Sol 34.6, Fable 34.0, Terra 20.8, Sonnet 14.6, Luna 14.3), splits routine shell from hostile-environment work, and changes the Operator's *escalation* rung to Opus 5 rather than Sol-max — the opposite of what all three reports prescribe. The plan under critique cites TB 2.1 alone and inherits the reports' ordering, including the escalation rung. The brief made live research mandatory where the reports are "thin, stale, or contradictory," and near-saturation is the textbook case.

**6. First-hand repository defects rather than asserted risk.** Their delta row proposing a single runtime-neutral registry argues that separately materialized rosters invite "responsibility, effort, and review-policy drift" — a correct argument with no instance attached. There is a live instance in the tree, which I verified again while writing this critique: `.claude/agents/architect-claude-xhigh.md` carries `model: opus` while its master `packs/codex/agents/architect-claude-xhigh.md` carries `model: fable`, so raising the effort tier silently changes vendor; and the two architect lanes have asymmetric tool surfaces (the master Claude tiers carry no `WebSearch`/`WebFetch` while the other lane is launched with web search enabled via `.mcp.json`). My draft cites those, plus the duplicated `executor-heavy-xhigh` role and the `OPENAI_API_KEY` lane of Finding 12. The ground-truth instruction was to verify against the tree rather than assume; their argument would be strictly stronger with the evidence that exists.

**7. Context shape as an enforced dispatch constraint.** My draft gives every role a declared maximum context shape (`packet` / `scoped` / `subsystem` / `repo` / `haystack`) that the dispatcher enforces at cast time, precisely because an agent cannot be trusted to refuse context it has already received. Theirs expresses the same insight as a prose prohibition on "more than approximately 100,000 relevant tokens," which is not evaluable before the search that discovers relevance (Finding 9).

### Convergences the synthesizer should treat as settled

Six decisions were reached independently by both drafts, which is the strongest signal this exercise can produce, and I would resist re-litigating any of them: retire `executor-heavy` / `executor-heavy-xhigh` as effort-named roles and make effort a cast property; cast Opus 5 rather than Fable 5 as the standing orchestrator on the dossier's alignment evidence, inverting the repository's current MODE A/B ordering; split the read-only detective from an execution-licensed runtime investigator; make the no-self-family rule a computed function of recorded author family rather than a convention a Director must remember; cast Gemini 3.7 Flash narrowly into multimodal intake only, behind an activation probe, with Antigravity's model-level quota opacity carried as a stated assumption; and derive OpenAI allowance weights from the vendor's published per-window message ranges rather than from per-token price tables.

Where the two drafts genuinely diverge, the divergence is about *how much the independence mandate should cost and who pays when a pool empties* — theirs takes the harder line on independence and does not price it; mine prices it and permits a stated, logged degradation. That is the one substantive question the merge has to decide, and it is decidable: Finding 8's probe would settle it with about a week of measurement on the installed tier.
